Curtz's denominator conjecture: OEIS A189733, revision 12, COMMENTS, May 23, 2011

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

How can repeatedly adding fractions produce denominators with a simple repeating pattern? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang explain why the array behind Paul Curtz’s denominator conjecture has exactly such a pattern, and use the same method to establish related identities and correct several assertions. They explicitly credit Gao’s earlier generating-function proof of the central result, rather than presenting that result as previously unproved.

The construction starts with a square array whose rows and columns are numbered from zero. Every diagonal entry is zero; immediately above the diagonal, row m contains the reciprocal of m+1, positive for even m and negative for odd m. Each remaining upper entry is the sum of the entry immediately to its left and the entry one row down and one column left. These prescribed boundary values take precedence over the addition rule. The question concerns the first row: after reducing each fraction, what denominator remains? Zero is assigned denominator one. Curtz proposed six formulas in 2011 in the online integer-sequence catalogue entry A189733.

The authors obtain the fractions themselves, not merely their denominators. For every nonnegative integer k, the six first-row entries starting at index 6k are (0, 1/(3k+1), 1/(2k+1), 1/(3k+2), 0, −1/(6k+6)). Thus the row begins 0, 1, 1, 1/2, 0, −1/6. Every displayed nonzero fraction is already reduced, so its denominator can simply be read off; the two zeros contribute denominator one. The reduced numerators repeat 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, −1, with least period six. The denominator sequence itself is not periodic: each of its six residue-class subsequences is an arithmetic progression, possibly constant.

The proof explains where six comes from. The authors replace the array calculation with polynomials satisfying a matching recurrence. Integrating these polynomials over the unit interval reproduces the reciprocal boundary values, because integrating a power gives the reciprocal of one more than its exponent. A generating function—a power series recording the first-row entries as coefficients—then packages all these integrals together. Algebra turns it into the difference of two logarithmic series, divided by the series variable. Before that division shifts the indices, one series contributes only at multiples of three and the other only at multiples of two. Their overlap and alternating signs produce the six cases. For example, index five receives −1/2 and 1/3, giving −1/6. This is coefficientwise formal algebra, not an argument requiring analytic convergence.

The explicit row also yields the denominator recurrence and generating function previously proved by Gao, including the expression recorded empirically by Colin Barker. It settles Barker’s related recurrence and generating-function conjectures for the quotient obtained by dividing the row index plus one by the reduced denominator, and establishes numerator identities recorded by W. I. Hurt. Extending the array below its diagonal gives a reflection rule and an alternating binomial-sum identity. A companion array, formed by taking twice the next column minus the current column, has similarly explicit diagonal fractions. Their reduction involves only a possible factor of three; the authors derive denominator formulas and a reciprocal sum, and correct a numerator-index shift. The entry −5/12 provides a concrete failure of the unshifted formula.

Removing the alternating signs from the boundary produces a different instructive outcome. The first-row value at index n becomes (Lₙ₊₁−1)/(n+1), where the Lucas numbers start with 2 and 1 and each later number is the sum of its two predecessors. The manuscript credits this expression to earlier contributions by R. Stephan and J. Jasinski. It then disproves the assertion that positive integer entries occur only at prime one-based ranks. At rank 705, which equals 3·5·47, the Lucas number L₇₀₅ leaves remainder one upon division by 705. A finite modular matrix calculation establishes this divisibility certificate, so the corresponding entry is a positive integer despite its composite rank. This counterexample is not a classification of all integer ranks.

A further result explains denominators along an antidiagonal, meaning entries whose row and column indices have a fixed sum. For arbitrary rational boundary seeds and zero diagonal, those upper entries are obtained from the relevant seeds by an integer triangular transformation with ones on its diagonal. Its inverse also has integer entries, so both collections have exactly the same common denominator-clearing integers. For either reciprocal boundary used here, the least common multiple of the reduced denominators is therefore the least common multiple of 1 through the number of strict upper-antidiagonal entries.

The evidence has distinct scopes. The manuscript supplies written proofs for its universal assertions. It describes a pinned Lean source proving the six denominator formulas for every nonnegative block index, with an internal theorem establishing the six rational values; this does not extend formal coverage to all the corollaries. A separate exact-arithmetic checker compares the main recurrence and formulas through row index 240 and checks other identities on stated finite ranges. Those checks are not universal proofs. The contribution is a unified explanation and extension of the credited earlier work, not a general theory of all such arrays. The manuscript leaves informal divisor and “super-autosequence” descriptions untreated because it specifies no precise identities for them.
